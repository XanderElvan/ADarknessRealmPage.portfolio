Songwriting 1: Writing To Music

This two-part prompt covers some of the basics of songwriting, and writing to music, using the example instrumental provided below. This prompt assumes no prior knowledge. If you already have some songwriting experience, please feel free to follow your own process while listening to the instrumental.

You can pause the audio at any time, using the Play / Pause button. You can also use the Rewind button to restart the track, or drag the progress slider to replay specific sections.

Finding Initial Ideas In Music

One of the best ways to find initial lyrics is to follow your instincts, either visually or melodically.
If working visually, click play on the Example Instrumental below, and as you listen, note down your initial responses. For example:

  • - Do you see any pieces of scenery, or shades of colour in your head?
  • - Does the music trigger any memories for you?
  • - Do you feel drawn to a particular time of day, or season?


Alternatively, you can respond to the melody by humming along. As you do so, see if any words or phrases start to take shape. A series of hums can often reveal the endings of phrases. If this approach works for you, you may want to start a voice recording, or pause the track to note anything down.

Once you have found a few phrases, you’re ready to move onto Part Two.

If you would like some example phrases to take into Part Two, you can use or adapt any of the following:

  • - He / She loves me.
  • - What would the wind say?
  • - It’s time I change.
  • - Was this another mistake?

Turning Ideas Into Lyrics

Song lyrics do not have to rhyme, but for this exercise we’re going to build lyrics around your chosen phrases using a combination of perfect rhyme and half rhyme, two of the most common rhyme types in songwriting.

Perfect Rhyme refers to words with identical sounds after the opening vowel. For example:

Is it ringing any bells? Like you’ve been here before?
I couldn’t even tell, I’ve never felt so sure.

Half Rhyme is more subtle, requiring only the final consonant sounds to rhyme. For example:

Is it ringing any bells? Like you’ve been here before?
I couldn’t even tell, I’ve never felt so sure.


By using both, you combine the clarity of perfect rhymes with softer ones, which prevent your lyrics becoming forced or cliché.
As a starting point, take one of your phrases and write 2-4 lines following the above template: combining the two rhyme types in an AB, AB rhyme scheme.
If that feels too restrictive, break away from it- try incorporating one perfect rhyme to start or end a series of half rhymes, or use a more complex rhyme scheme.

Once you have a few lyrics, try humming or singing them along to the instrumental. Syllable counting will be covered in future prompts, alongside more advanced structures and techniques, as you work towards a finished song. The aim for now is to get more comfortable writing lyrics to music.
